# Real Costs and Wages in the U.S.

## Overview
This project explores how different college degree fields relate to financial pressure when paying for essential household necessities—specifically **food** and **childcare**. Using state-level earnings by degree field, state-level center-based childcare costs, and a USDA-style food cost benchmark, this analysis estimates the **share of income consumed by necessities** in a scenario representing a **single-income household with two young children**.

Rather than classifying outcomes as “affordable” or “unaffordable,” the analysis focuses on **cost burden** (the percentage of income spent on food and childcare) because major expenses such as housing, transportation, taxes, healthcare, insurance, and debt are not included in this capstone.

---

## Main Questions
1. How do **earnings differ by degree field** across states?
2. How large is the **combined food + childcare cost burden** relative to earnings?
3. Which degree fields show the **highest necessity cost burden**?

---

## Key Findings (Summary)
- Degree fields with lower earnings show a **higher share of income** consumed by food and childcare.
- Even higher-earning degree fields can experience significant financial pressure depending on childcare costs.
- The results highlight how quickly basic necessities can consume income before accounting for other required expenses.

## How the Scenario is Modeled
- **Household framing**: one income with **two young children** requiring childcare.
- **Childcare**: summed cost of **Infant + Toddler** center-based care by state.
- **Food**: USDA low-cost plan monthly value converted to annual and scaled to approximate a household with a parent and children.
- **Metric used**:  
  **necessity_cost_pct = (annual_food_cost + annual_child_care_cost_two_children) / annual_earnings**)
